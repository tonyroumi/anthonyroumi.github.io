Learning Mechanisms

Adversarial Learning

Overview

The goal of adversarial networks is to learn a usable training signal from samples of two distributions, then use that signal to make one distribution resemble the other.

Formally, let \(P_*\) denote a reference distribution and let \(P_\theta\) denote the distribution induced by a parameterized model. We would like to choose \(\theta\) so that

\[ P_\theta \rightarrow P_*. \]

The core value that this framework provides is that the model is no longer limited to optimizing a fixed, hand-specified notion of similarity or probabilistic fit. Instead, it can learn a training signal from the data and capture generalized structure or behavior that may be difficult to obtain otherwise.

Derivation

The core GAN derivation in this section follows the video in the cited YouTube lecture, with portions of the mathematical explanation expanded upon. [1] [3]

Reference and model distributions

Let \(p_*(x)\) be the reference probability density and \(p_\theta(x)\) be the model probability density.

Introduce a discriminator

\[ D_\Theta(x) \in [0,1], \]

interpreted as the probability that \(x\) came from \(p_*\) rather than \(p_\theta\). Equivalently, for a binary label \(Y\in\{0,1\}\),

\[ D_\Theta(Y\mid x) = \begin{cases} D_\Theta(x), & Y=1 \quad \text{(real)},\\[4pt] 1-D_\Theta(x), & Y=0 \quad \text{(generated)}. \end{cases} \]

The target conditional label distribution is deterministic:

\[ p_b^*(Y\mid x) = \begin{cases} 1, & Y=1,\; x\sim p_*(x),\\ 0, & Y=0,\; x\sim p_*(x),\\ 0, & Y=1,\; x\sim p_\theta(x),\\ 1, & Y=0,\; x\sim p_\theta(x). \end{cases} \]

We have therefore defined two conditional Bernoulli distributions. The discriminator produces \(D_\Theta(Y\mid x)\), while \(p_b^*(Y\mid x)\) assigns the desired class label. We train the discriminator to align these two distributions.

From KL divergence to binary cross-entropy

One way to express this alignment is through the KL divergence:

\[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p_b^* \,\|\, D_\Theta\right) = \mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p_b^*(Y\mid x)} \left[ \log \frac{p_b^*(Y\mid x)} {D_\Theta(Y\mid x)} \right]. \]

Expanding the expectation gives

\[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p_b^* \,\|\, D_\Theta\right) = \sum_Y p_b^*(Y\mid x)\log p_b^*(Y\mid x) - \sum_Y p_b^*(Y\mid x)\log D_\Theta(Y\mid x). \]

The first term does not depend on the discriminator parameters, so minimizing the KL divergence with respect to \(\Theta\) is equivalent to minimizing the cross-entropy

\[ \mathcal{L} = -\sum_Y p_b^*(Y\mid x)\log D_\Theta(Y\mid x). \]

Because there are only two classes,

\[ \mathcal{L} = -p_b^*(0\mid x)\log D_\Theta(0\mid x) -p_b^*(1\mid x)\log D_\Theta(1\mid x). \]

Substituting \(D_\Theta(1\mid x)=D_\Theta(x)\) and \(D_\Theta(0\mid x)=1-D_\Theta(x)\) gives the standard binary cross-entropy loss:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}} = -\left(1-p_b^*(1\mid x)\right)\log\!\left(1-D_\Theta(x)\right) - p_b^*(1\mid x)\log D_\Theta(x). \]

Averaging over real and generated samples gives the discriminator loss

\[ \mathcal{L}_D = -\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_*} \left[\log D_\Theta(x)\right] - \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta} \left[\log\left(1-D_\Theta(x)\right)\right]. \]

If the model is represented by a generator \(G_\theta\) that transforms \(z\sim p_z\) into a sample \(G_\theta(z)\), then

\[ \mathcal{L}_D = -\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_*} \left[\log D_\Theta(x)\right] - \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_z} \left[ \log\left(1-D_\Theta(G_\theta(z))\right) \right]. \]

The adversarial objective

The discriminator attempts to distinguish samples from \(p_*\) and \(p_\theta\), while the generator attempts to make its generated samples difficult to distinguish from real samples. These are directly competing objectives.

Define the GAN value function

\[ V(G,D) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_*}\left[\log D(x)\right] + \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_z} \left[ \log\left(1-D(G(z))\right) \right]. \]

The discriminator maximizes this value while the generator minimizes it. This is the original GAN minimax objective. [1] [2]

\[ \boxed{ \min_G \max_D V(G,D) }. \]

This gives a two-player minimax game. In the idealized setting, training can be viewed as searching for a Nash equilibrium: a state in which neither player can improve its own objective by changing its parameters while the other player remains fixed.

Optimal discriminator for a fixed generator

For a fixed generator, write the value function directly in terms of the reference and model densities:

\[ V(G,D) = \int \left[ p_*(x)\log D(x) + p_\theta(x)\log(1-D(x)) \right]dx. \]

Because \(D(x)\) can be optimized pointwise, we maximize

\[ p_*(x)\log D(x) + p_\theta(x)\log(1-D(x)) \]

for each \(x\). Taking the derivative with respect to \(D(x)\),

\[ \frac{d}{dD(x)} \left[ p_*(x)\log D(x) + p_\theta(x)\log(1-D(x)) \right] = \frac{p_*(x)}{D(x)} - \frac{p_\theta(x)}{1-D(x)}. \]

At a stationary point,

\[ \frac{p_*(x)}{D(x)} - \frac{p_\theta(x)}{1-D(x)} = 0. \]

Solving for \(D(x)\) gives

\[ \boxed{ D_G^*(x) = \frac{p_*(x)} {p_*(x)+p_\theta(x)} }. \]

The second derivative is

\[ -\frac{p_*(x)}{D(x)^2} - \frac{p_\theta(x)}{(1-D(x))^2} < 0, \]

so this stationary point is a maximum. [1] [2]

Optimal generator under the optimal discriminator

Now assume that the discriminator remains optimal while the generator changes. Define

\[ C(G) = \max_D V(G,D) = V(G,D_G^*). \]

Substituting the optimal discriminator,

\[ C(G) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_*} \left[ \log \frac{p_*(x)} {p_*(x)+p_\theta(x)} \right] + \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta} \left[ \log \frac{p_\theta(x)} {p_*(x)+p_\theta(x)} \right]. \]

Let

\[ m(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(p_*(x)+p_\theta(x)\right). \]

Then

\[ C(G) = -\log 4 + D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p_*\,\|\,m\right) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p_\theta\,\|\,m\right). \]

Using the Jensen-Shannon divergence,

\[ D_{\mathrm{JS}}(P\|Q) = \frac{1}{2} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(P\,\|\,M\right) + \frac{1}{2} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(Q\,\|\,M\right), \qquad M=\frac{1}{2}(P+Q), \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{ C(G) = -\log 4 + 2D_{\mathrm{JS}}\!\left(p_*\,\|\,p_\theta\right) }. \]

The Jensen-Shannon divergence is non-negative and reaches its minimum value of zero if and only if the two distributions are equal. Therefore, [1] [2]

\[ p_\theta=p_* \]

is the global optimum, and at that point

\[ D_G^*(x)=\frac{1}{2}. \]

The minimum value of the GAN objective is

\[ \min_G C(G) = -\log 4. \]

Thus, in the idealized setting, minimizing the generator objective while maintaining an optimal discriminator pushes the model distribution toward the reference distribution.

Idealized Training Dynamics

The derivation suggests the following conceptual progression in an idealized GAN training process:

  1. Initial state: \(p_\theta\) and \(p_*\) differ, and the discriminator is not yet optimal.
  2. Discriminator update: for the current generator, \(D\) moves toward \(D_G^*(x)\).
  3. Generator update: the generator changes \(p_\theta\) so that it moves closer to \(p_*\).
  4. Ideal convergence: \(p_\theta=p_*\), at which point \(D(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}\) everywhere the distributions have support.

This reasoning assumes that the discriminator can remain near its optimum as the generator changes. Real training generally does not satisfy that assumption exactly.

Training Issues

1. An overly strong or overly weak discriminator

Overly strong discriminator

The theoretical analysis often assumes that the discriminator approaches \(D_G^*\) rapidly. In practice, however, a discriminator that becomes too good can stop providing a useful learning signal to the generator.

For the original saturating generator objective

\[ \mathcal{L}_G = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_z} \left[ \log\left(1-D(G_\theta(z))\right) \right], \]

let \(D(x)=\sigma(a(x))\), where \(a(x)\) is the discriminator logit. Then, for a sample \(z\),

\[ \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_G = -D(G_\theta(z)) \, \nabla_x a(G_\theta(z)) \, \nabla_\theta G_\theta(z). \]

If the discriminator can almost perfectly identify generated samples, then

\[ D(G_\theta(z))\approx 0, \]

and consequently

\[ \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_G \approx 0. \]

The generator then receives very little gradient information. This is the classic vanishing-gradient problem associated with the original saturating GAN generator objective. [2]

Overly weak discriminator

The opposite problem occurs when the generator changes too quickly for the discriminator to keep up. A poorly trained discriminator provides an inaccurate training signal, so a bad discriminator can in turn produce bad generator updates.

GANs are therefore usually trained with alternating optimization: 

for k discriminator steps:
    update discriminator parameters using ∇L_D

update generator parameters using ∇L_G

The relative number of discriminator and generator updates, as well as their learning rates, must be balanced. In some settings the discriminator is trained more aggressively, for example with

\[ \operatorname{lr}(D) > \operatorname{lr}(G), \]

although the appropriate balance depends on the architecture, objective, and data.

2. Non-convergence

Even if both players receive meaningful gradients, adversarial optimization does not behave like ordinary minimization of a single scalar objective. The coupled gradient dynamics can cycle around an equilibrium rather than converge to it.

Consider a simple two-player game with scalar parameters \(x\) and \(y\):

\[ L_A(x,y)=xy, \qquad L_B(x,y)=-xy. \]

Their gradients are

\[ \frac{\partial L_A}{\partial x}=y, \qquad \frac{\partial L_B}{\partial y}=-x. \]

The game has an equilibrium at

\[ x=y=0. \]

However, alternating or simultaneous gradient updates can produce rotational dynamics around the origin. Instead of approaching the equilibrium, the parameters can trace a stable orbit around it. This simple example illustrates why the existence of a Nash equilibrium does not imply that ordinary gradient-based GAN training will actually converge to that equilibrium.

3. Mode collapse

Mode collapse occurs when the generator learns to achieve good discriminator performance by mapping many different latent samples to the same, or a very small number of, outputs. Instead of using the latent variable \(z\) to represent diverse outcomes,

\[ z \sim p_z(z) \quad \longrightarrow \quad G_\theta(z) \approx x_0 \]

for many distinct values of \(z\). In the extreme case, the generator effectively ignores the latent input and produces nearly the same sample every time.

The generated output may still look realistic, so the generator can appear to perform well on individual samples. However, it has failed at the actual distribution-matching objective: \(p_\theta\) captures only a small portion of the support or modes of the reference distribution \(p_*\).

This behavior can arise because repeatedly producing an output that is known to fool the discriminator can temporarily reduce the generator's loss. The generator therefore has an incentive to concentrate probability mass around a small set of successful outputs rather than representing the full diversity of the data distribution.

\[ p_\theta(x) \not\approx p_*(x) \qquad \text{even when individual samples from } p_\theta \text{ appear realistic.} \]

In other words, sample quality alone is not sufficient. Successful generative modeling requires both realistic samples and adequate coverage of the modes of the target distribution. [1]
